## Before you read

#### Thesis

<p class="thesis">Asad argues that “there cannot be a universal definition of religion” (p. 29). What counted as religion has always depended on particular forms of power and discipline, and the wish to define religion as a transhistorical essence is itself a product of post-Reformation Christian and liberal history. Reading Clifford Geertz’s famous definition clause by clause, he shows that it cuts religion off from power and reduces it to meaning and private belief, and he asks instead: “How does power create religion?” (p. 45).</p>

#### Context you need

- **Geertz’s definition.** “Religion as a Cultural System” (1966) gave anthropology its standard definition of religion, in five clauses: symbols, moods and motivations, a general order, an aura of factuality, and realism (pp. 29–30). Asad’s bare numbers in parentheses, such as (90), are pages of Geertz’s 1973 reprint.
- **Genealogy.** A method from Nietzsche, later Foucault: trace how a concept that now looks natural was put together, and what it made possible. Foucault is not named in this chapter, but its language of discipline and of power producing truth is close to his.
- **Augustine and medieval discipline.** Augustine came to defend imperial force against the Donatists as corrective teaching (p. 34). The medieval Church formed Christians through monastic rules, penance and compulsory confession.
- **Natural Religion.** During the wars of religion, Lord Herbert of Cherbury looked for a religion common to all humanity; Locke and Kant carried the idea forward (pp. 40–42).

#### Watch for

- Which clause of Geertz’s definition each section takes on: symbols (pp. 30–32), moods and motivations (pp. 32–35), general order (pp. 35–39, 43–46), factuality and belief (pp. 46–48), “uniquely realistic” (pp. 49–52).
- The refrain of power creating religious truth (pp. 33, 35, 45).
- Belief against practice: belief as an inner state that comes before knowledge is the modern assumption under attack.
- The footnotes, which carry most of the medieval evidence (nn. 14–22, 29).

## <span class="num">1</span> Context in depth

### Geertz and interpretive anthropology

Clifford Geertz (1926–2006) led interpretive, or symbolic, anthropology. Trained at Harvard under Talcott Parsons and drawing on Max Weber, he treated culture as a public system of meanings carried by symbols, which the anthropologist interprets much as a critic reads a text. “Religion as a Cultural System” appeared in 1966 and was reprinted in *The Interpretation of Cultures* (1973), as Asad notes (p. 29). It became the standard definition because it seemed to solve several problems at once: it applied to any society, it needed no reference to gods, and it took religion seriously as meaning instead of reducing it to social structure (Durkheim’s heirs) or to illusion (Marx, Freud). Parsons divided action into separate cultural, social and personality systems, and Geertz’s essay assumes that division: hence Asad’s phrase “resort to Parsonian theory” (p. 32).

### Natural Religion and deism

The wars of religion that followed the Reformation, ending with the Peace of Westphalia in 1648, split Latin Christendom. Edward, Lord Herbert of Cherbury, in *De veritate* (1624), proposed five common notions that all people hold by reason: there is a supreme God; he ought to be worshipped; virtue and piety are the chief part of worship; sins must be repented; and there are rewards and punishments after this life. He is often called the father of English deism. The deists who followed, such as John Toland and Matthew Tindal, held that this religion of reason sufficed, so that revelation could at most republish it. Asad’s point (pp. 40–42) is that this turned religion into propositions that could be compared across peoples.

### Augustine and the Donatists

The Donatist church arose in North Africa after the persecution under Diocletian (303–305). It rejected bishops who had handed over the scriptures to the authorities, held their sacraments invalid, and kept its own rival bishops. Augustine, bishop of Hippo from the 390s to 430, at first wanted only argument. Within a few years of the imperial edict against the Donatists in 405 he was defending state coercion, citing the parable in which the master tells his servant to compel the guests to come in (Luke 14). The same view of grace preparing an unwilling will shaped his later case against Pelagius. Asad takes the episode from Peter Brown’s biography (pp. 34–35).

### Monastic and penitential discipline

In the early Middle Ages the Rule of St Benedict governed Western monastic life, and “religious” meant a monk (p. 39, n. 22). Penitentials, handbooks that fixed a penance for each sin, spread from Irish and British monasteries to the Continent (p. 37, n. 14). The Lateran Council of 1215 made annual confession to one’s own priest compulsory (p. 38, n. 18). These are the institutions behind Asad’s “authorizing processes”: regimes that taught, examined, corrected and punished.

### Genealogy as method

Nietzsche’s *On the Genealogy of Morality* (1887) traced moral values not to a timeless origin but to histories of conflict and power. Michel Foucault took up the term in the 1970s for histories, such as *Discipline and Punish* (1975), that show disciplines and institutions producing the subjects and truths they claim only to describe. A genealogy does not say what a thing essentially is; it asks how a concept came to seem natural and what it made possible. Foucault is not named in this chapter (he appears in Ch. 3, p. 115). The Introduction (p. 1) calls the genealogy of religion a central theme of the book.

## <span class="num">2</span> Argument map

<div class="argument" markdown="1">

1. **Essence and separation (pp. 27–28).** Twentieth-century anthropology made religion a distinct space “which cannot be reduced to any other” (p. 27). Defining that essence suits the liberal demand that religion be kept apart from politics, law and science: for secular liberals a strategy of confinement, for liberal Christians one of defence (p. 28).
2. **The stake (pp. 28–29).** The separation of religion from power is “a modern Western norm” (p. 28). Applied to Muslim traditions, it makes religious discourse in politics look like a disguise for power.
3. **Thesis (p. 29).** No universal definition of religion is possible. Geertz’s essay is the starting point for finding the historical shifts that made religion look like a transhistorical essence.
4. **Symbols and dispositions (pp. 30–35).** Geertz treats symbols as meaning-carrying objects outside social practice. Asad asks how some symbols come to be authoritative, and finds in Augustine that power, not symbols, creates the conditions for religious truth.
5. **Authorisation (pp. 35–39).** Practice and discourse about practice are different operations. What ties a disposition to a cosmic order is an authorising process. The medieval Church did that work; modern churches handed it over to individual belief.
6. **The early modern turn (pp. 40–43).** The universal concept of religion answered seventeenth-century Christian problems. Herbert, Locke and Kant made religion a matter of propositions and an essence behind its historical forms.
7. **Meaning and belief (pp. 43–48).** Geertz’s demand that religion affirm something echoes the missionary’s. His Problem of Meaning and his priority of belief over knowledge reproduce the private place post-Enlightenment society allows Christianity.
8. **Perspective, ritual and method (pp. 48–54).** Religion is now optional as science is not. Ritual cannot produce faith without prior discipline. Geertz’s two-stage method is one operation, and the task is to unpack “religion” historically, not to define it better.

</div>

## <span class="num">3</span> Section by section

### Opening: essence, liberalism and Geertz <span class="pp">pp. 27–30</span>

Nineteenth-century evolutionists such as Fustel de Coulanges (n. 1) saw religion as an early condition from which law, science and politics later separated. Louis Dumont (pp. 27–28) pictures medieval religion as “a great cloak” that became an individual affair with the modern state; Asad notes that he still assumes one essence across both epochs. Asad sets aside Geertz’s work on Indonesia and Morocco and quotes the definition: a religion is “(1) a system of symbols which act to (2) establish powerful, pervasive, and long-lasting moods and motivations in men by (3) formulating conceptions of a general order of existence and (4) clothing these conceptions with such an aura of factuality that (5) the moods and motivations seem uniquely realistic” (pp. 29–30).

### The concept of symbol as a clue to the essence of religion <span class="pp">pp. 30–35</span>

**Symbol (pp. 30–32).** Geertz calls a symbol a vehicle for a conception, then treats it as the conception itself (the number 6, however written). Drawing on Vygotsky, for whom a child’s intellect develops by internalising social speech, Asad treats symbols as complexes formed in practice and asks how some come to be “established as natural or authoritative as opposed to others” (p. 31). Geertz’s “models of” and “models for” reality looks promising, but the two levels mirror each other so neatly that it is hard to see “how social change can ever occur” (p. 32).

**Moods and motivations (pp. 32–35).** No one can predict a modern Christian’s dispositions from worship, because social, political and economic institutions shape them (p. 33). Brown’s Augustine (pp. 34–35) supplies the counter-case: free choice prepared by imposed *eruditio* and *admonitio*; “Let constraint be found outside; it is inside that the will is born”; persecution as a divinely “controlled catastrophe”. Asad lists the forms this power took: imperial and church law, sanctions from hellfire to good repute, institutions from family to church, and the body’s fasting, prayer, obedience and penance (p. 35).

### From reading symbols to analyzing practices <span class="pp">pp. 35–39</span>

Geertz says the symbols that induce religious dispositions and those that place them in a cosmic framework are the same. “But it does surprise!” (p. 36). His examples, awe from mana rather than the Grand Canyon, asceticism aimed at nirvana rather than weight-reduction, presuppose an authorising process (pp. 36–37). The notes show the medieval Church at that work: penitentials condemning vows at springs and trees while Gregory the Great urged taking over pagan temples (n. 14); papal commissioners in 1307 cross-examining witnesses to Thomas Cantilupe’s miracles (n. 15); annual confession (n. 18); the Franciscans made an Order, the Beguines suppressed (n. 19). The Church sought not uniformity but “the subjection of all practice to a unified authority” (p. 38).

### The construction of religion in early modern Europe <span class="pp">pp. 40–43</span>

Basil Willey presents Herbert as seeking a belief to command “the universal assent of all men as men” (p. 40), prompted by exploration and trade. Missionaries met the problem in practice: the Jesuits in China called ancestor reverence social, not religious, and the quarrel over the rites lasted a century (n. 24). Attention moved from God’s words to God’s works, so that in Locke, as Norman Sykes puts it, Revelation had to present itself “as a republication of Natural Religion” (p. 41). By 1795 Kant could say that “there can only be one religion which is valid for all men and at all times” (p. 42). The change belonged to “a new kind of state, a new kind of science, a new kind of legal and moral subject” (p. 43).

### Religion as meaning and religious meanings <span class="pp">pp. 43–48</span>

**Affirmation (pp. 43–45).** Geertz says a religion must “affirm something” (p. 43). Asad hears the missionary in this: the unevangelised either affirm nothing, so theory must read their “mute ritual hieroglyphics,” or affirm something that can be dismissed (p. 43).

**The Problem of Meaning (pp. 45–46).** For Geertz, chaos threatens at the limits of analysis, endurance and moral insight (p. 45). On this view any consoling philosophy would count as religion, and religion comes to resemble Marx’s ideology, “producing no knowledge” (p. 46). The bishop of Javols, who told Auvergne peasants that there is no religion in a swamp, shows the older view (n. 29).

**Belief (pp. 46–48).** Belief changes with its objects: medieval Christians valued pain as participation in Christ’s suffering, while modern Catholics fight it as Christ the Healer did (pp. 46–47). For twelfth-century Christians belief rested on knowledge of doctrine, canon law, confession, shrines and saints (p. 47). Among Needham, Southwold and Harré, Asad prefers Harré: belief is a mental state confined to people with certain institutions (p. 48).

### Religion as a perspective <span class="pp">pp. 48–52</span>

Geertz sets the religious perspective against common-sense, scientific and aesthetic ones. A long note disputes the accounts of science and art, pointing to experiment in Christian asceticism and to John Berger on cubism (n. 32). On ritual, Geertz says conviction is “somehow generated” (p. 50); Asad replies that the will to adopt a religious standpoint must already be present. Following Alfred Schutz, Geertz has believers make “Kierkegaardian leaps” (p. 51) and return changed. The result is a paradox: the common-sense world is universal yet altered by religious experience, while the religious world is never touched by common sense (pp. 51–52).

### Conclusion <span class="pp">pp. 53–54</span>

Asad turns Geertz’s method into questions: can a symbol’s meaning be fixed apart from its form of life, or from the disciplines that secure its correct reading? “Is the concept of religious training entirely vacuous?” (p. 53). He anticipates a charge of elite bias, that he ignores heterodox peasants and decentralised traditions such as Hinduism, and answers that he is not proposing a better definition (p. 54). Tambiah’s “special awareness of the transcendent” shows that the search for one continues (n. 36).

## <span class="num">4</span> Close reading

### No universal definition <span class="pp">p. 29</span>

> “I want to get at this well-known fact while trying to avoid a simple nominalism. … Nevertheless, what the anthropologist is confronted with, as a consequence, is not merely an arbitrary collection of elements and processes that we happen to call ‘religion.’ For the entire phenomenon is to be seen in large measure in the context of Christian attempts to achieve a coherence in doctrines and practices, rules and regulations, even if that was a state never fully attained. My argument is that there cannot be a universal definition of religion, not only because its constituent elements and relationships are historically specific, but because that definition is itself the historical product of discursive processes.”
> <span class="src">Asad · p. 29</span>

The *well-known fact* is that medieval and modern religion differ in their forms, preconditions and effects. A *simple nominalism* would conclude that “religion” is only a word stuck on unrelated things. Asad refuses that: the medieval elements do hang together, not because they share an essence but because an institution worked to make them cohere. Unity is produced by authority, and never fully achieved.

The last sentence gives two reasons, and they differ in kind. The first is empirical: the elements and their relations vary by period. A careful definer could absorb that by abstracting further, which is just what Herbert and Kant did. The second blocks the move. A definition of religion is not a neutral observation; it is *itself the historical product of discursive processes*, a move inside the history it claims to describe. Defining religion therefore has consequences: it can confine religion, defend it, or tell missionaries what to look for. The sentence presupposes that concepts are made by institutions and arguments, so that the anthropologist’s definition is one more voice in that history, not a view from outside it.

### Authorizing processes <span class="pp">p. 37</span>

> “… appears plausible, but only because it presupposes a question that must be made explicit: how do authorizing processes represent practices, utterances, or dispositions so that they can be discursively related to general (cosmic) ideas of order? In short, the question pertains to the authorizing process by which ‘religion’ is created.”
> <span class="src">Asad · p. 37</span>

What appears plausible is Geertz’s claim that a disposition is religious because it has a place in a cosmic framework: awe before mana, not before the Grand Canyon. Asad grants the link and asks how it is made. *Represent* is the key verb. Practices do not carry their cosmic meaning on their face; someone must describe them, so that a fast becomes a penance or a vow at a spring becomes paganism. *Discursively related* means connected by talk: sermons, canons, confessors’ questions. *Authorizing* names who has the standing to make those connections stick. The second sentence ends on *created*: “religion” is not found and then interpreted, but made by the process that sorts practices into religious and not, true and false. The passage presupposes the distinction drawn on p. 36, between discourse in practice and discourse about practice.

## <span class="num">5</span> Key terms

<div class="glossary" markdown="1">

Religion (Geertz)
:   The five-clause universal definition: a system of symbols producing moods and motivations through conceptions of a general order clothed in factuality (pp. 29–30).

Symbol
:   For Geertz, a vehicle for a conception, its “meaning” (p. 30). For Asad, a set of relationships formed in social practice, whose authority must be explained (p. 31).

Models of / models for
:   Geertz’s double aspect of culture patterns, which both mirror reality and shape it (p. 32).

Moods and motivations
:   The two dispositions Geertz says religious symbols produce; moods look back to their conditions, motivations forward to their ends (p. 33).

Authorizing process / discourse
:   The institutional work that makes practices count as religion, true or false, sacred or profane (pp. 37–39). The chapter’s central concept.

*disciplina*
:   Augustine’s corrective discipline, “teaching by inconveniences” (*per molestias eruditio*), with *eruditio* (teaching) and *admonitio* (warning) (p. 34).

Natural Religion
:   Herbert’s religion common to all, made of beliefs, worship and ethics; for Asad a crucial step towards the modern idea of religious belief (pp. 40–42).

Problem of Meaning
:   Geertz’s threat of chaos at the limits of analysis, endurance and moral insight, which religious symbols answer (p. 45).

Belief
:   For Geertz, a state of mind that comes before knowledge. For Asad, historically variable: in the twelfth century it was built on knowledge and embodied in practice (pp. 46–48).

Religious perspective
:   Geertz’s religious way of seeing, set against common-sense, scientific and aesthetic ones (pp. 48–49).

Two-stage operation
:   Geertz’s method: analyse the meanings of symbols, then relate them to social and psychological processes. Asad: the two are one (p. 53).

</div>

## <span class="num">6</span> Who’s who

<div class="people" markdown="1">

Clifford Geertz
:   The foil. His 1966 definition is read as a modern, privatised Christian view that inadvertently takes “the standpoint of theology” (p. 43).

Augustine of Hippo
:   The counter-model: coercion as a condition of truth, discipline as its maintenance (pp. 34–35); he returns in the argument on ritual (p. 50).

Peter Brown
:   Historian of late antiquity; Asad’s source on Augustine (pp. 34–35), shrines and Javols (nn. 16, 29).

Louis Dumont
:   French anthropologist whose medieval “cloak” still assumes one essence of religion (pp. 27–28).

Lord Herbert · Locke · Kant
:   Herbert seeks a common denominator of all religions (p. 40, via Willey); Locke reduces Christianity towards Natural Religion (p. 41, via Sykes); Kant posits one religion behind many confessions (p. 42).

Vygotsky
:   Soviet psychologist: intellect develops by internalising social speech (p. 31).

Parsons · Schutz · Kierkegaard
:   Behind Geertz’s split between cultural and social levels (p. 32) and his shifting perspectives and “leaps” (p. 51).

Needham · Southwold · Harré
:   Debate on whether belief is a universal mental state; Asad prefers Harré (p. 48).

Marx · Tylor · Durkheim · Robertson Smith
:   Passing mentions: religion as ideology (p. 46); whether any tribe lacks religion (n. 25); religion’s “own truth” (p. 50); belief replacing knowledge (n. 23).

Radcliffe-Brown · Douglas · Tambiah · Evans-Pritchard
:   Cited in notes: rites inducing sentiments, an approach Geertz recalls (n. 9); religion as explanation (n. 28); religious experience as spiritual, not social (n. 35); a new definition (n. 36).

## <span class="num">8</span> Cross-chapter connections

- **Introduction (pp. 1–2).** Anthropologists of Islam need to know “how ‘religion’ has come to be formed as concept and practice in the modern West” (p. 1). Chapters 1 and 2 sketch religion’s emergence as a modern object; Chapters 3 and 4 take up pain and self-abasement, medieval elements modern religion rejects.
- **Ch. 2 (pp. 62, 78–79).** A parallel genealogy of “ritual”. Rites as “apt performance” under the Rule of St Benedict (p. 62) and Hugh of St Victor’s sacraments for humiliation, instruction and exercise (p. 78) extend this chapter’s Augustine. Geertz’s *model of* / *model for* returns through Denny on Islamic ritual (p. 79).
- **Ch. 3 (pp. 83, 115).** Power “works to produce truthful discourses” in judicial torture and penance (p. 83). The Lateran decree on confession (n. 18 here) returns (p. 115), and the medieval valuing of pain (p. 46 here) becomes the topic.
- **Ch. 4 (pp. 129–31).** Geertz’s rituals and palaces as “texts to be read” (p. 129) meet Asad’s reply that “the reading is a product of social discipline” (p. 131): the monastic case for the two stages being one.
- **Ch. 5 (pp. 171–72).** “The translation of cultures” as anthropology’s task; compare the closing call to unpack what the anthropologist “translates as ‘religion’” (p. 54).
- **Ch. 6 (pp. 205, 233–34).** The modern state required “the forcible redefinition of religion as belief” (p. 205), and liberals hold that morality, with religious belief, is “essentially a personal matter” (p. 233). Geertz’s essay on ideology in Indonesia is criticised (pp. 233–34).
- **Ch. 7 (pp. 259–60).** Multiculturalism’s split between a public domain and private ones, where religion is housed, fails: public practices keep structuring the private.
