## At a glance

#### Thesis

<p class="thesis">“When Asian women struggle to free themselves from patriarchy and from the blinders of traditional male Western theologies, they see new visions of liberating Christologies” (p. 7). The book tests this claim across India, South Korea, the Philippines and Hong Kong, then offers the author’s own christology.</p>

#### The author

- Muriel Orevillo-Montenegro is a Filipina Protestant theologian who taught at the Divinity School of Silliman University in Dumaguete (pp. xiii, 1).
- The book grew from her doctoral dissertation at Union Theological Seminary, New York, where James H. Cone was her “main mentor and tough critic” (pp. xi–xii). Her work among Philippine fisherfolk, farmers and the urban poor shaped her questions (p. xi).

#### Suggested reading order

Read the Prologue and Chapter 1 first, for the vocabulary; then any of the case studies in Chapters 2–4. Read Chapter 5 after at least one of them, since it critiques them. End with the Epilogue, the author’s own proposal.

</section>

## <span class="num">1</span> The argument in one page

<div class="argument" markdown="1">

1. **The colonial Christ (Prologue; Ch. 1).** Christianity returned to Asia with empire. Its Jesus judged Asia’s religions and served conquest, so Christology must be reinterpreted (pp. 7, 11).
2. **Men’s methods fall short (Ch. 1).** Adaptation, inculturation, contextualisation and interreligious dialogue each improve on the colonial Christ. All neglect gender, and most neglect either poverty or Asian religions. “No man can truly speak for women in Asia” (p. 48).
3. **India (Ch. 2).** Under caste, dowry and poverty, women read Jesus’ blood with their own and name the Christ as Shakti, the creative feminine power of Hindu tradition (pp. 72–79).
4. **Korea (Ch. 3).** From comfort women, factory women and Confucian patriarchy, women image Jesus as the shaman (*mudang*) who releases *han*, as mother, and as Woman Messiah (pp. 97–111).
5. **Philippines (Ch. 4).** Women turn the colonial *pasyon* into a language of resistance, walk the passion in the protest march, and see Jesus as “fully liberated” human. The author pushes towards the many-breasted Mother (pp. 125–56).
6. **Hong Kong (Ch. 5).** Kwok Pui-lan’s hybrid, organic, Wisdom Christ is set against Wong Wai-Ching’s postcolonial critique of the whole liberationist project. The author answers Wong but adopts her “subject-in-process” (pp. 162–92).
7. **The author’s christology (Epilogue).** Christhood is the Spirit’s anointing for liberating, life-giving acts. Jesus is its paradigm, but “the Christ is larger than the historical Jesus” (p. 198). Her image of it is *Inang Bayan*, the Mother who is land and people.

</div>

## <span class="num">3</span> Threads to follow across chapters

- **Jesus and the Christ.** Pieris: “Jesus is Christ, but not all of Christ is Jesus” (p. 47). The distinction recurs in Shakti christology (p. 78), in Christ seen in suffering women (pp. 150–52), in Kwok’s Christ “one and many” (p. 182), and in the Epilogue’s Christhood (pp. 194–98). It is the book’s central theological move.
- **Jesus’ maleness.** Each chapter answers it differently: humanity, not maleness (p. 57); “largely a Western feminist concern” (p. 104); “accidental” (p. 145); a non-issue for Chinese women, against Wong’s charge of avoidance (pp. 165, 188); transcended in the Christ (pp. 79, 150).
- **The cross and suffering.** The book rejects imposed, glorified suffering (pp. 8, 108, 127) yet keeps returning to suffering chosen in resistance (pp. 108, 139). The cross is named the overarching unfinished task (p. 199).
- **Goddess and Wisdom figures.** Shakti (Ch. 2), Kuan-yin and Sophia–Prajna (Chs. 3, 5), the *babaylan* and Mebuyan (Ch. 4), and *Inang Bayan* (Epilogue). Each is a feminine image placed beside or inside the Christ.
- **Story as method.** Testimony, poems, songs and liturgy are treated as theological sources (pp. 6, 92, 146).
- **Colonialism and empire.** From the Prologue’s “rifles and bibles” to Chapter 5’s postcolonial theory. The test, borrowed from Dirlik, is whether theory still produces resistance (p. 162).

## <span class="num">4</span> Core vocabulary

Each term is defined fully in the chapter companion listed.

<div class="glossary" markdown="1">

Cosmic / metacosmic · colonial Christ · inculturation · contextualisation
:   Pieris’s map of Asian religions and the methods of male Asian christology (Ch. 1).

Dalit · *Shakti* · *Satchitananda*
:   Caste outsiders; divine feminine power; the Vedantic triad read as Trinity (Chs. 1–2).

*Minjung* · *han* · *han-pu-ri* · *mudang*
:   The oppressed people; accumulated grief; its ritual release; the shaman (Chs. 1, 3).

*Pasyon* · *lakbayan* · *babaylan*
:   Lenten passion chant; the protest march; the indigenous priestess (Ch. 4).

Hybridity · subject-in-process · strategic essentialism
:   Postcolonial terms used by Kwok and Wong (Ch. 5).

Christhood / the christic · *Inang Bayan*
:   Anointing for liberating action, shared beyond Jesus; Mother as land and people (Ch. 1, p. 48; Epilogue).

</div>

## <span class="num">5</span> Questions for the whole book

<p class="note">The companion’s own assessment, not the author’s.</p>

- **What is the criterion?** The book judges christologies by whether they liberate (pp. 7, 82, 192). It praises release from “the Nicea-Chalcedon formula” (p. 82) yet sometimes argues on Chalcedonian lines (p. 69). Is liberating function enough to judge a christology?
- **Description or norm?** Is the thesis a finding about what Asian women write, or a standard that decides which writers count?
- **Who speaks?** Most voices are educated theologians; grassroots women usually arrive quoted second-hand. Filipina domestic helpers appear in three chapters, but they never speak for themselves.
- **Essentialism.** “Asian women,” “the poor woman” and motherhood images carry the argument. Chapter 5’s Wong Wai-Ching is the book’s own internal critic on this point.
- **Whose heritage?** Reclaiming shamanism, Shakti or Mebuyan raises the question of appropriation (Yamashita, Ch. 3, pp. 102–3). The question is posed but not settled.
